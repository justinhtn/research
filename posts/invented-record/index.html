Tracing how a fine-tuned model uses what it learned

Summary

I wanted to know what happens to knowledge a language model learns by fine-tuning: whether it holds it, whether its reasoning reaches it, and what it says the knowledge came from. A Qwen3-14B model was fine-tuned on two weeks of a simulated drug-screening lab where every true result is known. Its reasoning was measured sentence by sentence and word by word, by restarting it many times from each point and by swapping single words and their internal state. The model holds what it learned, but whether it reaches it can turn on one word, and a word’s effect sets into the text within a few dozen tokens. When it does reach the answer, the record it cites is usually real but from the wrong place.

I asked a fine-tuned model the same question three times and got two different answers. The question was about one compound in a simulated drug-screening lab: what is the AUC of CX-11661 on line ME-685, the average share of cancer cells still alive across eight doses (lower means the compound kills more)? It could choose one of three ranges or “cannot be known from the lab’s records”. Its prompt held the lab’s handbook and the question, and no plate records at all.

The first time it searched the handbook, found nothing and said the answer could not be known. The second time it gave the right answer, 0.5 to 0.8, the range the lab actually measured.

The answer was real. The source it gave for it was not: it said it read the range from a section called “Plates”, and no such section exists anywhere in its prompt.

So the answer came from somewhere other than the record it cited. That leaves two questions, which the rest of this post follows through its reasoning. When the model gets the answer right, where does the answer actually come from? And when it says “cannot be known”, how does it get there, and is the knowledge still in there?

How it learned the lab

The lab is simulated, so the truth of every experiment is known. Each plate tests one compound at eight doses on one line of cancer cells and records how many cells survive. The model learned two busy weeks of it: 35 plates covering 26 compounds, 15 of them new.

The model is Qwen3-14B, an open model that thinks out loud before it answers. I left its own weights alone and fine-tuned a small add-on instead (a LoRA adapter, under 2% of the model’s size). The teacher was the same model with the lab’s notes in front of it. It graded every word the fine-tuned model wrote, and over 538 lessons the fine-tuned model learned to write as the teacher did without the notes. The fine-tuned model also carries a second add-on, trained earlier, that makes it cautious about anything untested.

Without the add-ons and without the notes, the same model gets 2.5% of these questions right, where guessing would get about 20%. Whatever the fine-tuned model gets right, then, came from its training.

Where the answer is decided

To see where in its reasoning the answer is settled, I stopped it after each sentence and let it finish sixteen times from there. The share of those finishes that end right is its chance of getting it right from that point. Here and below, that chance is about where the model ends up (the right answer, “cannot be known” or a wrong option), not about which words it writes. The method comes from work on whether a model’s reasoning is faithful (Lanham et al. 2023) and on forks in generated text (Bigelow et al. 2024).

Both traces start with about a one-in-four chance. The one that ends right jumps inside a single sentence, the one in which it writes that there is a section called “Plates”. By the words “multiple entries” it has gone from 2 in 16 to 16 in 16, and it stays there. The one that ends “cannot” slides down instead, which is the second question, taken up below.

This was the clearest of twelve questions it answered both ways, and Figure 3 shows all of them.

In ten of the twelve traces that ended right, the chance jumps within a sentence or two, at the point where it starts writing out a record as if reading one. A jump marks when the answer was decided and is silent on why, much as a win-probability line leaps at a goal without telling you who made the pass.

One plate is right. The other is a real plate from the day after, which tested other compounds on another line. The value it gives, 0.59, is the average of the two real values, 0.56 and 0.63. It has put real pieces in the wrong places, which memory researchers call misattribution (Schacter 2022).

The section it read from has a source as well. While the teacher was training the model, the teacher’s notes carried a section headed “CURVES NAMING CX-11661”. The model never saw it, yet what it describes looks like it, and in another trace it calls it the “Curves” section.

The pattern holds across the roughly 2,900 plates it cited in all its saved answers. It names a plate of the right compound about four times as often as chance would, but the exact right plate only 6 to 10% of the time, so most of its citations are real lab plates attached to the wrong question.

A record that isn’t real might still be what carries the model to its answer. I tested that on 100 of its traces, rewriting the sentence where the record begins and letting the model finish 32 times from each version. Taking the record out barely mattered: the share answering right went from 57% to 64%. The record travels with the answer without carrying it.

Landing on “cannot be known”

The first answer is the other half of the puzzle: the model said it could not know something it had learned. None of the traces that ended this way turned at a single point. In the lower panel of Figure 3, each one slides, usually from a low start, as the model checks its prompt for records and finds none.

Being told there was nothing to find was enough to send it there. In the same 100 traces, replacing the start of the record with “there are no plate records here” dropped the share answering right to 9%. The knowledge was still underneath. About a quarter of the time (27%) the model wrote out a record anyway, and when made to answer straight after the denial, it still leaned toward the right range: 37% of its answers other than “cannot”, against 25% by chance.

A single word can do as much. In one trace it writes “Wait, the user hasn’t provided the specific data…”. From just before that word it answers right 56% of the time. If the next word is “provided”, that becomes 59%; if it is “hasn’t”, a word it was only 13% likely to write, it becomes 22%. A second run gave the same drop.

When a word can no longer be undone

A word like “hasn’t” reaches what comes after it by two routes. One runs through the next words it leads the model to write. The other runs through the state the network stores for that word, which every later word reads back from. To find when it stops mattering, I swapped each route for its “provided” version, right at the word and again after the model had written a few more words (2, 8 and 32 tokens, where a token is a word or part of one). Each test was 48 runs, each matched with an unchanged run that used the same random choices.

  • At the word itself, either route on its own flips most of the outcome.
  • Two tokens later, swapping the word does nothing, because what it carried has already moved into the words written after it.
  • Up to 8 tokens later, changing how those later words took it in can still flip the outcome.
  • By 32 tokens no change inside the network makes a difference, and what the model has written now holds the decision.

A second fork (“on BR” against “on plate”) sets in the same way. A third (“the data shows” against “might”) was still open at 32 tokens. In the two that set, the decision passed from the word into the text within about 32 tokens.

What I make of it

Fine-tuning is how people routinely put knowledge into models. It was already known that writing out a memory first helps a model use what it learned in pre-training (Sun et al. 2022), and that the reasoning a model states can misreport what produced its answer (Turpin et al. 2023; Lindsey et al. 2025). This case shows both where every detail can be checked against the truth.

What came apart here are three things that are easy to blur: whether the model holds something, whether its reasoning reaches it, and where it says it came from. People are like this too. We rebuild memories rather than replay them, and we often attach a real detail to the wrong source (Johnson, Hashtroudi & Lindsay 1993). The records the model cites say more about how it learned than about where its answer came from. And whether it gets there at all can turn on a few words, which training might be able to change.
